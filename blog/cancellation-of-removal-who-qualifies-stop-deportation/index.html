Cancellation of Removal Explained: Who Qualifies and How It Can Stop Deportation

November 16, 2026

Facing removal proceedings can be one of the most stressful experiences an immigrant and their family will ever face. However, receiving a Notice to Appear in immigration court does not necessarily mean deportation is inevitable. Depending on a person's immigration history, length of residence in the United States, criminal record, family relationships, and other circumstances, there may be forms of immigration relief available.

One important form of relief is cancellation of removal.

Cancellation of removal is a defense that may allow certain immigrants who are already in removal proceedings to ask an immigration judge to cancel their removal from the United States. For some nonpermanent residents, a successful case can also result in becoming a lawful permanent resident, or green card holder.

Cancellation of removal cases are complex. Simply living in the United States for many years or having U.S. citizen children does not automatically qualify someone for relief. Applicants must satisfy specific requirements under U.S. immigration law and convince the immigration judge that their case deserves approval.

This guide explains cancellation of removal in 2026, including the difference between cancellation for green card holders and nonpermanent residents, the 10-year rule, qualifying relatives, exceptional and extremely unusual hardship, criminal issues, evidence, and what happens if an application is approved.

What Is Cancellation of Removal?

Cancellation of removal is a form of discretionary relief available in immigration court.

In simple terms, an immigrant who is facing removal asks an immigration judge to allow them to remain in the United States despite the government's attempt to remove them.

There are different forms of cancellation of removal, but two of the most important are cancellation of removal for lawful permanent residents, generally filed using Form EOIR-42A, and cancellation of removal for certain nonpermanent residents, generally filed using Form EOIR-42B.

The requirements are very different.

For a qualifying lawful permanent resident, approval generally means the person keeps lawful permanent resident status.

For a qualifying nonpermanent resident, approval results in adjustment of status to lawful permanent residence.

Importantly, cancellation of removal is generally a defense pursued in immigration court. It is not simply an application that someone without immigration court proceedings files with USCIS because they have lived in the United States for 10 years.

Cancellation of Removal for Nonpermanent Residents: The 10-Year Rule

One of the most frequently misunderstood concepts in immigration law is what people sometimes call the 10-year immigration law or 10-year green card rule.

There is no general rule saying that anyone who has lived undocumented in the United States for 10 years automatically receives a green card.

Instead, certain nonpermanent residents who are in removal proceedings may qualify for cancellation of removal under Section 240A(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act.

Generally, the applicant must establish:

  • At least 10 years of continuous physical presence in the United States
  • Good moral character during the required period
  • No disqualifying convictions under the applicable provisions of immigration law
  • Removal would cause exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to a qualifying U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, or child
  • The applicant deserves cancellation of removal as a matter of discretion

Meeting the 10-year requirement alone is therefore not enough.

Understanding the 10 Years of Continuous Physical Presence

An applicant generally must demonstrate continuous physical presence in the United States for at least 10 years.

Evidence may include tax returns and IRS transcripts, employment records, pay stubs, rental agreements, mortgage statements, utility bills, bank statements, medical records, school records, birth certificates of children born in the United States, insurance records, church or community records, vehicle registrations, official correspondence, and affidavits from people with personal knowledge of the applicant's residence.

An immigration attorney may organize these records chronologically to demonstrate that the applicant was physically present throughout the required period.

The Stop-Time Rule

Calculating continuous physical presence can become complicated because immigration law contains what is commonly called the stop-time rule.

Certain events can stop the accumulation of the required period of residence or physical presence. The timing and legal sufficiency of a Notice to Appear, as well as certain criminal offenses, can therefore become extremely important.

This is one reason an attorney should examine the person's entire immigration and criminal history rather than simply counting backward 10 years from today's date.

What Does Good Moral Character Mean?

A nonpermanent resident seeking cancellation of removal must also demonstrate good moral character during the required statutory period.

Immigration judges may examine many aspects of the person's history.

Evidence supporting good moral character can include consistent employment, payment of taxes, caring for children or relatives, community participation, religious or volunteer involvement, letters of support, and compliance with legal obligations.

Certain criminal conduct or other statutory circumstances can prevent an applicant from establishing good moral character.

Applicants should never assume that an arrest, citation, conviction, dismissed case, expungement, or old criminal matter is irrelevant.

Immigration law treats criminal offenses differently from state criminal law. A conviction that appears relatively minor under California law, for example, can potentially have serious immigration consequences.

What Is "Exceptional and Extremely Unusual Hardship"?

For many applicants, this is the most difficult part of a cancellation of removal case.

The law requires more than showing that deportation would be emotionally or financially difficult.

The applicant generally must establish that removal would result in exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to a qualifying relative.

The immigration judge evaluates the circumstances cumulatively rather than necessarily looking at each hardship factor in isolation.

Who Is a Qualifying Relative?

For regular non-LPR cancellation of removal, the qualifying relative must generally be the applicant's U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, or child.

This distinction is critical.

An applicant's own hardship is generally not enough by itself. The focus is primarily on the hardship experienced by the qualifying relative.

Other relatives may still be relevant to the overall circumstances, but not every family member qualifies for purposes of the statutory hardship requirement.

Examples of Hardship Evidence

Every case is different, and there is no single document that proves exceptional and extremely unusual hardship.

A strong cancellation case may involve several interconnected factors, including medical conditions, disabilities, special educational needs, psychological circumstances, financial dependency, caregiving responsibilities, and conditions in the country to which the applicant could be removed.

An attorney may present medical records, evaluations, school documents, financial evidence, expert reports, country-condition evidence, declarations, and testimony to explain the complete impact of removal.

Case Study Example #1: U.S. Citizen Child With Medical Needs

Consider a hypothetical father named Carlos.

Carlos entered the United States more than 15 years ago and has remained here continuously. He has no disqualifying criminal convictions and has consistently worked and paid taxes.

Carlos has two U.S. citizen children. One child has a significant medical condition requiring ongoing treatment by specialists.

If Carlos is placed into removal proceedings, his attorney might evaluate cancellation of removal.

But the argument should not simply be: "My child will miss me."

Instead, the case could document exactly how Carlos's removal would affect the child's treatment, insurance coverage, transportation to appointments, finances, daily care, and access to appropriate specialists.

The attorney might also analyze what would happen under both realistic possibilities: the child remaining in the United States without Carlos or relocating abroad with him.

The cumulative evidence could be critical.

Case Study Example #2: Twenty Years in the United States Is Not Enough by Itself

Imagine María has lived in California for 22 years.

She works, pays taxes, and has never been arrested. She is placed in removal proceedings and believes that her 22 years in the United States automatically qualify her for cancellation of removal.

They do not.

If María does not have a qualifying U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, or child who would experience the required level of hardship, she may not satisfy the statutory requirements for regular non-LPR cancellation.

This demonstrates why the phrase "10-year green card" can be misleading.

Ten years is one requirement, not the entire case.

Case Study Example #3: Financial Hardship Combined With Other Factors

Suppose José has lived in the United States for 17 years and has a U.S. citizen spouse and two U.S. citizen children.

José provides most of the family's income.

If he were removed, the family might lose its home and health insurance. One child also receives specialized educational services, while José's spouse has a medical condition that limits her ability to work full-time.

Financial difficulty by itself may not necessarily establish the required hardship.

However, immigration judges can consider relevant hardship factors together. The combination of medical, educational, financial, emotional, and caregiving circumstances can create a much more detailed hardship claim.

Cancellation of Removal for Green Card Holders

Lawful permanent residents can also face removal proceedings.

A green card does not make someone immune from deportation.

Certain criminal convictions, immigration violations, and other circumstances can cause DHS to place a lawful permanent resident into removal proceedings.

Cancellation of removal for permanent residents has different requirements from the 10-year rule discussed above.

Generally, an LPR seeking cancellation must establish that the person has been a lawful permanent resident for at least five years, has continuously resided in the United States for at least seven years after being admitted in any status, has not been convicted of an aggravated felony, and deserves cancellation of removal as an exercise of discretion.

The application is generally made using Form EOIR-42A.

Criminal Convictions and Cancellation of Removal

Criminal history can completely change an immigration case.

For lawful permanent residents, an aggravated felony conviction can make someone ineligible for LPR cancellation of removal.

For nonpermanent residents, certain convictions can also prevent eligibility.

The immigration definition of an aggravated felony is technical and does not necessarily correspond with how a crime is labeled under state law.

Anyone facing immigration court after an arrest or conviction should obtain qualified legal advice before making assumptions about eligibility.

Discretion: Qualifying Does Not Guarantee Approval

Cancellation of removal is discretionary relief.

That means proving the statutory requirements does not automatically guarantee that an immigration judge will approve the application.

The judge may evaluate favorable and unfavorable factors.

Positive factors can include long residence in the United States, family ties, employment history, tax compliance, rehabilitation, community service, property ownership, and evidence of good character.

Negative factors may include criminal history, immigration violations, dishonesty, repeated legal problems, or other unfavorable conduct.

The presentation of the entire case therefore matters.

What Evidence Should Be Included?

Strong cancellation cases are often document-intensive.

Depending on the circumstances, evidence may include immigration records, passports, entry documents, tax returns, employment records, school records, medical documentation, psychological evaluations, financial records, property records, declarations, criminal dispositions, and letters of support.

Country-condition evidence may also become important when the case involves what would happen if qualifying relatives relocated abroad.

The objective is not simply to submit a large quantity of paperwork. The evidence should tell a clear and legally relevant story.

What Happens at the Individual Hearing?

Eventually, a cancellation of removal applicant may have an individual or merits hearing before an immigration judge.

The applicant may testify under oath and be questioned about immigration history, residence, family, employment, criminal history, and other relevant circumstances.

The government attorney may cross-examine the applicant and challenge eligibility or evidence.

Other witnesses may testify as well.

The immigration judge then determines whether the applicant satisfies the legal requirements and whether relief should be granted as a matter of discretion.

Because testimony becomes part of the immigration court record, preparation is extremely important.

What Happens If Cancellation of Removal Is Approved?

The result depends on the type of cancellation.

If an immigration judge grants cancellation of removal to an eligible lawful permanent resident, the person's removal is canceled, and the individual generally retains lawful permanent resident status.

If cancellation of removal is granted to an eligible nonpermanent resident, the person's status is adjusted to that of a lawful permanent resident.

In other words, successful non-LPR cancellation can result in obtaining a green card.

Can You Apply for Cancellation of Removal Without Being in Immigration Court?

Cancellation of removal is generally relief requested during removal proceedings before an immigration judge.

Someone should not intentionally place themselves into removal proceedings simply because they have lived in the United States for 10 years and believe they qualify.

Doing so can create significant immigration risk.

Before taking any action involving immigration authorities, an individual should have an immigration attorney evaluate the complete case.

Cancellation of Removal vs. Asylum

Cancellation of removal and asylum are separate forms of immigration relief.

Asylum generally focuses on persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution based on protected grounds under U.S. immigration law.

Cancellation of removal focuses on requirements involving residence or physical presence, qualifying relatives, hardship, criminal history, good moral character, and discretion, depending on the category.

Some people in immigration court may potentially have more than one form of relief available.

Can Cancellation of Removal Stop Deportation?

When granted, cancellation of removal can stop the removal order and allow the individual to remain legally in the United States.

But every case depends on its specific facts.

Two immigrants who have each lived in the United States for 15 years can have completely different outcomes because one may have qualifying relatives and substantial hardship evidence while the other may not. Criminal history, prior immigration proceedings, departures from the United States, and other factors can also change eligibility.

This is why cancellation of removal should never be viewed simply as a "10-year rule."

It is a complex form of deportation defense that requires careful legal analysis.

Frequently Asked Questions About Cancellation of Removal

What is cancellation of removal?

Cancellation of removal is a form of relief that may allow certain immigrants in removal proceedings to avoid deportation. Depending on the type of cancellation, an approved applicant may retain lawful permanent resident status or become a lawful permanent resident.

What is the 10-year rule for cancellation of removal?

Certain nonpermanent residents may need at least 10 years of continuous physical presence in the United States. However, living in the United States for 10 years does not automatically qualify someone for a green card. Other requirements must also be satisfied.

Can I get a green card after living in the United States for 10 years?

Not automatically. A person seeking non-LPR cancellation generally must be in removal proceedings and satisfy additional requirements involving good moral character, criminal history, qualifying relatives, hardship, and discretion.

Does having a U.S. citizen child stop deportation?

No. Having a U.S. citizen child does not automatically prevent deportation. In a non-LPR cancellation case, the applicant generally must demonstrate that removal would cause exceptional and extremely unusual hardship to a qualifying U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, or child.

Who qualifies as a relative for cancellation of removal?

For regular non-LPR cancellation of removal, qualifying relatives generally include a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse, parent, or child.

Can a green card holder be deported?

Yes. Lawful permanent residents can be placed in removal proceedings under certain circumstances, including certain criminal convictions and immigration violations.

Can a green card holder apply for cancellation of removal?

Potentially. Lawful permanent residents have a separate form of cancellation of removal. Eligibility generally includes requirements involving five years as an LPR, seven years of continuous residence after admission in any status, and no aggravated felony conviction.

What is Form EOIR-42B?

Form EOIR-42B is the application generally used for cancellation of removal and adjustment of status for certain nonpermanent residents.

What is Form EOIR-42A?

Form EOIR-42A is generally used by certain lawful permanent residents seeking cancellation of removal.

What does "exceptional and extremely unusual hardship" mean?

It is a heightened hardship standard used in non-LPR cancellation cases. Ordinary emotional or financial difficulties associated with family separation may not be enough. Immigration judges examine the specific circumstances affecting qualifying relatives and consider relevant factors together.

Can a criminal record prevent cancellation of removal?

Yes. Certain criminal convictions can make an applicant legally ineligible for cancellation of removal. The immigration consequences of a conviction can be different from its treatment under state criminal law.

Do I need an immigration lawyer for cancellation of removal?

Immigration law does not require every person to have an attorney, but cancellation of removal cases can involve complex questions involving physical presence, criminal history, hardship, evidence, testimony, and immigration court procedures. Legal representation can help applicants understand the requirements and prepare their cases.
